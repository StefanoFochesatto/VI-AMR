\begin{abstract}
FIXME Free-boundary problems posed as variational inequalities, including obstacle problems, appear in many scientific and engineering applications.  In their finite element (FE) solution, localization of the free boundary may be a primary goal, and the geometrical localization error often dominates the overall numerical error.  In this paper we implement, using the Firedrake FE library, new parallel adaptive mesh refinement strategies which generate accurate, high-resolution free boundaries through $h$-refinement.  We evaluate three approaches: \emph{(i)} a tag-and-refine unstructured dilation operator method using discrete adjacency to the free boundary, \emph{(ii)} a tag-and-refine method based on variable-coefficient diffusion, which thresholds a diffused active-set indicator function, and \emph{(iii)} a metric-based mesh adaptation method which averages an anisotropic, Hessian-derived Riemannian metric with an isotropic metric computed from the diffused indicator in \emph{(ii)}.  For \emph{(i)} and \emph{(ii)} classical \emph{a posteriori} error estimators must be added within the computed inactive sets to attain convergence.  These methods are evaluated, versus mesh complexity, by norm error and by geometrical localization using Jaccard distances for active sets.  Applications include classical Laplacian obstacle problems and a shallow ice flow problem for predicting glaciation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Obstacle problems appear in many scientific, engineering, and economic applications \cite{FacchineiPang2003,KinderlehrerStampacchia1980,Rodrigues1987}.  In the unilateral case the solution is constrained to be above or below a given function, while solving a partial differential equation (PDE) whereever it is not in contact with that function.  Such a problem, which can be posed in weak form as a variational inequality (VI) \cite{KinderlehrerStampacchia1980}, extends the PDE model from fixed-location boundary conditions to allowing for free boundaries \cite{Friedman1982}, but the solution and the free-boundary geometry must be determined simultaneously.  Solvers are built on iterative optimization strategies because the constraints in these problems make them nonlinear even when based on a linear operator.

The finite element (FE) method was adapted to the solution of VI and obstacle problems by the 1970s; for examples see the \emph{a priori} estimate in \cite{Falk1974} or the well-known textbook by Ciarlet \cite{Ciarlet2002}.  The literature of FE solutions to VIs is now large, and we recall some this literature concerning \emph{a posteriori} estimators in Section \ref{sec:estimators}.  However, VI problems continue to represent challenging problems for solvers and meshing methods, and substantial developments have occurred in recent years, including \cite{BuelerFarrell2024,KeithSurowiec2024,KirbyShapero2024,PapadopoulosHintermuller2026}.  Rigorous results are often incomplete except for model problems.

This paper introduces the VIAMR\footnote{Download or clone the library from \href{https://github.com/StefanoFochesatto/viamr}{\texttt{github.com/StefanoFochesatto/viamr}}.} Python library for adaptive mesh refinement and adaptation on bilateral, scalar obstacle problems.  Its methods call the Firedrake \cite{Rathgeberetal2016} and PETSc \cite{petsc-user-ref} libraries for FE infrastructure and VI solvers, respectively.  In this Introduction we state the general case at which the new library is addressed, review some literature of \emph{a posteriori} estimators, and summarize the methods.

The unilateral Laplacian obstacle problem is the classical and canonical case, appearing in textbooks  \cite{Bueler2021,Ciarlet2002,Evans2010,KinderlehrerStampacchia1980} for example.  It finds the equilibrium vertical displacement $u$ of an elastic membrane, restricted to small deflections, over a domain $\Omega \subset \RR^d$.  The membrane is attached with displacement $g$ at the fixed boundary $\partial\Omega$ and it is subjected to an applied force $f$ over $\Omega$.  Additionally is it constrained to be above a given obstacle $\psi$, and the following complementarity problem \cite{FacchineiPang2003},
\begin{subequations} \label{eq:classical:ncp}
\begin{align}
  u - \psi \geq 0, \\
  -\nabla^2 u - f \geq 0, \label{eq:classical:ncp:b} \\
  (u - \psi)(-\nabla^2u - f) = 0 ,\label{eq:classical:ncp:c}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
applies almost everywhere over $\Omega$.

From a solution of system \eqref{eq:classical:ncp} we can identify certain subsets of $\Omega$.  The Poisson equation $-\nabla^2 u = f$, the \emph{interior equation} of the problem, holds on the \emph{inactive set} $E_u=\{x \in \Omega \,:\, u(x) > \psi(x)\}$, while $u=\psi$ on the \emph{active} or \emph{contact} set $C_u = \Omega \setminus E_u$.  The boundary between these, $\Gamma_u = \Omega \cap \partial E_u$, is called the \emph{free boundary}.  Determining these sets, which depend on $u$ and must be solved-for simultaneously with it, is usually a primary purpose of a numerical solution of the complementarity problem.

An example is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ball}, where the obstacle $\psi$ is an upper hemisphere and the active set $C_u$ (white-colored mesh) is a disc  (Note that $\psi$ is shown as a full sphere for aesthetic reasons only.)  The Poisson equation holds on $E_u$, the black-colored mesh, and the free boundary $\Gamma_u$ is a circle.  Both Dirichlet $u=\psi$ and Neumann $\partial (u-\psi)/\partial n = 0$ conditions apply along the free boundary $\Gamma_u$ \cite{Friedman1982}; intuitively-speaking this compensates for its unknown location.  The computation here used a single mesh; the mesh coloring is merely diagnostic.

A different physical interpretation of the classical obstacle problem \eqref{eq:classical:ncp} is that $u$ models the water pressure in a porous dam; the pressure cannot go below zero ($u\ge\psi=0$), and the goal is to determine which portion of the dam is saturated \cite{AinsworthOdenLee1993}.  In yet another physical interpretation, wherein the unilateral constraint is an upper bound ($u\le \psi$), the solution $u$ is the displacement in elastic/plastic torsion of a beam, and the goal is to determine which portion has failed plastically \cite{Kornhuber1994}.

As usual for an FE method, we replace the strong statement \eqref{eq:classical:ncp} with a weak formulation, and identify a function space for $u$.  That is, we write a VI over a Sobolev space \cite{Evans2010} as follows.  Let $\cX=H^1(\Omega)$ and suppose $\psi \in \cX \cap C(\bar\Omega)$ and $g \in C(\partial \Omega)$, with $g\ge\psi|_{\partial \Omega}$ for compatibility.  Define the closed and convex \emph{admissible subset}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:classical:admissible}
\cK = \{u \in \cX \,:\, u \ge \psi \text{ and } u|_{\partial \Omega} = g\}.
\end{equation}
For $f\in L^2(\Omega)$, the weak re-formulation of \eqref{eq:classical:ncp} seeks $u\in \cK$ so that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:classical:vi}
\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla(v - u) \ge \int_\Omega f(v - u) \quad \text{ for all } v \in \cK.
\end{equation}

Ignoring regularity and speaking intuitively, integration by parts yields $\big<\sigma, v-u\big>\ge 0$ for the residual $\sigma = -\nabla^2 u - f$.  This inequality, or \eqref{eq:classical:vi} itself, says that all vectors $v-u$, which point from the solution $u$ arbitrarily into $K$, are at most $90^\circ$ away from $\sigma$.  In particular, if $u$ is in the interior $\mathring\cK$ then $\sigma$ must be zero, and then $u$ solves the Poisson equation, and furthermore $E_u=\Omega, C_u=\emptyset, \Gamma_u = \emptyset$ in that case.  We see that \eqref{eq:classical:vi} generalizes the weak-form Poisson equation.

The obstacle problems addressed by the VIAMR library generalize the above classical obstacle problem.

\begin{libboxtitle}{VIAMR library scope:}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item scalar obstacle problems in which
  \item the constraint set is defined by upper and lower obstacles, and
  \item the operator can have any linear or nonlinear formula.
  \end{itemize}
\end{libboxtitle}

This general continuum problem is defined by certain data defined on a bounded, polygonal domain $\Omega\subset \RR^d$, including Dirichlet boundary values\footnote{Neumann boundary conditions along $\partial\Omega$, if any, are assumed to be part of the source term $\ell$ used in \eqref{eq:vi} below.} $g\in C(D)\strut$ over well-behaved $D\subset\partial\Omega$, and two continuous functions $\lb,\ub \in C(\bar\Omega)$, the \emph{box constraints}.  These data must satisfy compatibility conditions:
\begin{equation}
\lb < \ub \text{ on } \Omega \quad \text{and} \quad \lb \le g \le \ub \text{ on } D.\label{eq:compat}
\end{equation}
\noindent These data may be already be in FE spaces, or as Unified Form Language (UFL) symbolic expressions \cite{Alnaesetal2014}, but we assume continuity to enable nodal evaluation.  Unilateral constraints are chosen by setting the missing constraint to Python value \texttt{None}.

Let $\cX$ be a reflexive Banach space of scalar functions over $\Omega$ such that $W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)\cap C(\bar\Omega) \subset \cX \subset L^2(\Omega)$.  The closed and convex \emph{admissible subset} is defined as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:admissible}
\cK = \{v \in \cX \,:\, \lb \le v \le \ub \text{ and } v|_{D} = g\}.
\end{equation}
\noindent The compatibility conditions imply that $\cK$ is nonempty.  The set $\cK$ is bounded if $\lb,\ub$ are each bounded, but if one constraint is \texttt{None} then $\cK$ is a cone.

Let $\cX'$ denote the topological dual space, with pairing $\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>$.  The general problem covered by the library is to find $u\in \cK$ satisfying
\begin{equation} \label{eq:vi}
\left<F(u),v - u\right> \ge \left<\ell,v-u\right> \quad \text{ for all } v \in \cK,
\end{equation}
\noindent where $F:\cK \to \cX'$ is the (nonlinear) \emph{operator}, and a \emph{source} $\ell \in \cX'$ is separated for clarity;\footnote{Separating $\ell$ from the nonlinear operator $F$ is not strictly needed, as we may always write $\tilde F(v)=F(v)-\ell$ for a new operator, but it helps when considering how qualitative behavior relates to the data, and when comparing to the classical case \eqref{eq:classical:vi}.} both $F$ and $\ell$ are regarded as data provided to the library.  For example, $\left<F(u),w\right> = \int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla w$ and $\ell(w)=\int_\Omega f w$ in classical problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi}.  We assume $F$ is continuous, but otherwise general; it may only be defined for admissible inputs.

Let us say a bit more about continuum VI \eqref{eq:vi} before addressing its FE solution.  For $\cX = W^{1,p}(\Omega)$, this VI is well-posed if $F$ satisfies certain conditions from the theory of monotone operators.  Specifically, if $F$ is strictly-monotone, coercive, and continuous then \eqref{eq:vi} has a unique solution \cite[Corollary III.1.8]{KinderlehrerStampacchia1980}.  These conditions apply to the classical problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi} and its $p$-Laplacian extensions \cite{LiuBarrett1994}.  They also apply to the advection-diffusion problem in Section \ref{sec:results}, and, with appropriate adjustments \cite{JouvetBueler2012}, to flat-topography cases of the highly-nonlinear glacier geometry problem in Section \ref{sec:glacier}.  However, it is the user's responsibility to supply a well-posed problem to the library.

Now define the \emph{lower-} and \emph{upper-contact} (active) subsets, the \emph{inactive} set, and the \emph{free boundary}, respectively, for a solution $u\in\cK$ to \eqref{eq:vi}:
\begin{gather}
\label{eq:contactsets}
\Cbotu = \{x\in\Omega\,:\,u(x) = \lb(x)\}, \quad \Ctopu = \{x\in\Omega\,:\,u(x) = \ub(x)\}, \\
E_u = \Omega \setminus (\Cbotu \cup \Ctopu), \quad \Gamma_u = \Omega \cap \partial E_u, \notag
\end{gather}
Note that Figure \ref{fig:contactsets} illustrates a possibility.  Note that the contact sets are disjoint, by compatibility conditions \eqref{eq:compat}, and the inactive set may be disconnected even when $\Omega$ is connected.  The free boundary generally includes (disjoint) components from lower and upper contact.

Complementarity, illustrated by \eqref{eq:classical:ncp} for the classical problem, also applies to VI \eqref{eq:vi}.  Given that $\cK$ is defined by primal box constraints \eqref{eq:admissible}, at the solution the residual $\sigma=F(u)-\ell\in \cX'$, sometimes called the \emph{Lagrange multiplier}, has dual sign properties:\footnote{That is, $\left<\sigma,\varphi\right> \ge 0$ for every $\varphi \ge 0$ compactly supported in $\Omega \setminus \Ctopu$.  Prove this by substituting $v=u+\eps \varphi$ into \eqref{eq:vi}.  For sufficiently small $\eps>0$, such that $v\le \ub$ and thus that $v\in\cK$, then $\left<\sigma,v - u\right> = \eps \left<\sigma,\varphi\right> \ge 0$.  The second inequality is similar.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Fsign}
  \sigma \ge 0 \text{ on } \Cbotu \quad \text{and} \quad \sigma \le 0 \text{ on } \Ctopu,
\end{equation}
with $\sigma=0$ on their complement $E_u$, the inactive set where a PDE applies.  In fact, in standard cases with $\cX=W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ and $\ell\in L^1(\Omega)$, the residual $\sigma$ is a signed Radon measure \cite{EvansGariepy2015} which admits a decomposition $\sigma = \mu_{\bot,u} - \mu^{\top}_u$ into nonnegative measures supported on $\Cbotu$ and $\Ctopu$ respectively, e.g.~\cite[Theorem II.6.9]{KinderlehrerStampacchia1980}.

This Introduction has outlined the continuum problems which are in the scope of the VIAMR library.  Section \ref{sec:goals} will approximate these problems using piecewise-linear finite elements, and then consider the goals of \emph{a posteriori} estimators for VI problems, followed by the statements of existing estimators in Section \ref{sec:estimators}.  Section \ref{sec:viamr} describes the implemented library methods, some rigorous (with limited applicability) and some partly-heuristic (with general applicability).  Section \ref{sec:results} compares their performance on the well-studied classical obstacle model problem, and certain related uniformly-elliptic problems.  Some general methods are applied to a realistic glacier application in Section \ref{sec:glacier}.

\section{Finite element approximation and \emph{a posteriori} goals} \label{sec:goals}

The VIAMR library assumes that the piecewise-linear ($P_1$) FE method \cite{Ciarlet2002} has been applied to problem \eqref{eq:vi}.  Let $\cT_h$ be a (finite) shape-regular triangulation ($d=2$) or tetrahedralization ($d=3$) of $\Omega$ \cite{Braess2007}, and write $\cX_h$ for the finite-dimensional space of continuous functions which are piece-wise linear on each $T\in\cT_h$.  Regarding the Banach space $\cX$ we assume $\cX_h \subset \cX \cap C(\bar\Omega)$, so the method is conforming in that sense.  Denote the nodal interpolation operator by $I_h : C(\bar\Omega) \to \cX_h$

The discretized problem data are $g_h=I_h g$, $\lbh=I_h\lb$, and $\ubh=I_h\ub$.  By the nodal monotonicity property of $P_1$ elements, the same compatibility conditions as \eqref{eq:compat} hold, namely $\lbh < \ubh$ on $\Omega$ and $\lbh \le g_h \le \ubh$ on $D$, so we define the nonempty, closed, and convex \emph{discrete admissible set} just as in \eqref{eq:admissible}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:fe:admissible}
\cK_h = \{v_h \in \cX_h \,:\, \lbh \le v_h \le \ubh \text{ and } v_h = g_h \text{ on }D\}.
\end{equation}

Because $\cK_h \subset \cX_h \subset \cX$, one might suppose that we are constructing a conforming FE method for \eqref{eq:vi}.  However, by comparing definition \eqref{eq:admissible} we see that containment $\cK_h\subset \cK$ would depend on a certain pointwise relationship between the box constraints and their interpolants.  While ``$\cK_h \subsettilde \cK$'' applies generally, in some sense, we distinguish 3 precise levels of conformity:
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\roman{enumi})}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\cK_h \not \subset \cK$,
\item $\cK_h \subset \cK$,
\item $\cK_h = \cK \cap \cX_h$.
\end{enumerate}
For $P_1$ elements as here, if $\lb$ is somewhere concave or $\ub$ is somewhere convex then a mesh can be found which forces the least-conforming situation (i).  (This point was already made by Ciarlet \cite[Figure 5.1.3]{Ciarlet2002}.)  The better situation (ii) would apply under appropriate assumptions on the convexity of $\lb$ and $\ub$.   Alternatively (ii) could be imposed by applying monotone injection operators \cite{BuelerFarrell2024,GraeserKornhuber2009} to re-define $\lbh$, $\ubh$; see also \cite[proof of Theorem 5.1.2]{Ciarlet2002}.  The nicest situation (iii) holds if the continuum constraints $\lb$, $\ub$ are themselves affine.  The \emph{a posteriori} estimator literature for the classical obstacle problem often assumes this nicest case \cite[for example]{AinsworthOdenLee1993,BraessCarstensenHoppe2007,CarstensenHu2015}, as will certain examples in Section \ref{sec:results}, but a significant goal of our library is to provide methods which apply more generally.

The finite-dimensional FE approximation of \eqref{eq:vi} now seeks $u_h\in\cK_h$ satisfying
\begin{equation} \label{eq:fe:vi}
\left<F_h(u_h), v_h - u_h\right> \ge \left<\ell,v_h-u_h\right> \quad \text{ for all } v_h \in \cK_h.
\end{equation}
Note that if monotonicity and coercivity arguments, for example, show well-posedness for \eqref{eq:vi} then the same arguments presumably show it for \eqref{eq:fe:vi}, but here the operator $F_h:\cK_h\to\cX'$ may not be the restriction of $F$ in \eqref{eq:vi} to $\cK_h$.  It might be defined by a UFL expression for $F$, but quadrature and/or regularization may be used in its construction.  The VIAMR library generally allows these nonconforming situations to hold, namely $\cK_h \not \subset \cK$ and $F_h \ne F$, and Section \ref{sec:glacier} provides an example of both simultaneously.

Falk \cite{Falk1974} proved a classical \emph{a priori} bound for \eqref{eq:fe:vi} in the elliptic linear operator (Hilbert space) case, bounding the Sobolev norm $\|u_h-u\|_{W^{1,2}(\Omega)}$, thus extending Cea's lemma to VIs.  A further extension to permit coercive nonlinear operators is in \cite[Theorem 5.1]{Bueler2025}.  We state the latter general bound here because it illuminates the concerns of \emph{a posteriori} estimators, for nonlinear operators, as well.  We say that $F$ is $q$-coercive over\footnote{Reference \cite{Bueler2025} considers how to modify Theorem \ref{thm:abstractestimate} when $F$ is defined only on $\cK$.} $\cX$ if $\left<F(v)-F(w),v-w\right> \ge \alpha \|v-w\|_\cX^q$ for all $v,w \in \cX$, a definition which holds with $q=2$ for uniformly-elliptic linear operators, and $q=p$ for the $p$-Laplacian \cite{LiuBarrett1994}.  The proof of the following theorem permits situation (i) nonconformity, and it does not require the finite-dimensional operator $F_h$ to be continuous or $q$-coercive.

\begin{theorem} \label{thm:abstractestimate}  Suppose $u\in\cK$ solves \eqref{eq:vi} and $u_h\in\cK_h$ solves \eqref{eq:fe:vi}.  For $q>1$, assume that $F$ is Lipschitz on bounded subsets and $q$-coercive over $\cX$ with constant $\alpha>0$.  Let $\sigma=F(u)-\ell$ and $R_h=\max\{\|u\|,\|u_h\|\}$.  Then there is a constant $C>0$ depending on $R_h$ and $\alpha$, but not otherwise on $u$ or $u_h$, so that
\begin{align}
\|u-u_h\|_\cX^q &\le C \inf_{v_h\in\cK_h} \|v_h - u\|_\cX^{q'} \label{eq:apriori} \\
   &\,\quad + \frac{2}{\alpha} \Big(\inf_{v\in\cK} \left<\sigma,v-u_h\right> + \inf_{v_h\in\cK_h} \left<\sigma,v_h-u\right> + G(u_h)\Big) \notag
\end{align}
where $G(u_h) = \left<F(u_h)-F_h(u_h),u_h\right>$ and $q'=q/(q-1)$.
\end{theorem}

So, when can the norm error $\|u-u_h\|_\cX$ be large, according to this bound?  The first term, expected from Cea's lemma, can be made small by improving the approximation properties of the space $\cX_h$ in particular.  In this paper we will apply $h$-refinement for this, but see e.g.~\cite{KirbyShapero2024} for progress using Berstein polynomials of higher degree on a conforming-situation (iii) problem.  Regarding the next two terms, in large parentheses, recall that $\sigma$ is a measure supported in the contact set $C_u=\Cbotu\cup\Ctopu$.  The first term, with $\inf_{v\in\cK}$, can be taken to be zero whenever $u_h\in\cK$, including conforming situations (ii) and (iii).  However, the term will be positive if points in $\cK$ cannot ``reach'' $u_h$, at some locations in $C_u$, because a discrete obstacle locally blocks it away from the continuum obstacle.  The next term, with $\inf_{v_h\in\cK_h}$, is positive in the alternate situation where points in $\cK_h$ cannot reach $u$, at some locations in $C_u$, but it may also be nonzero for the usual FE approximation reason, that $u$ is not in $P_1$.  In the most-conforming situation (iii), this $\inf_{v_h\in\cK_h}$ term will presumably be zero away from the free boundary.  The final term $G(u_h)$ is zero if there are no quadrature or regularization errors made when applying to points in $\cK_h$, i.e.~when $F(u_h)=F_h(u_h)$, but otherwise its contribution is evidently operator-dependent.

Before addressing the goals of \emph{a posteriori} estimators in this context, note that solving \eqref{eq:fe:vi} for $u_h$ determines these subsets of $\Omega$:
\begin{gather}
\label{eq:fe:sets}
\Cbotuh = \{x\in\Omega\,:\,u_h(x) = \lbh(x)\}, \quad \Ctopuh = \{x\in\Omega\,:\,u_h(x) = \ubh(x)\}, \\
E_h = \Omega \setminus (\Cbotuh \cup \Ctopuh), \quad \Gamma_h = \Omega \cap \partial E_h. \notag
\end{gather}
Because the functions are continuous, the inactive set $E_h=\{x\,:\,\lbh(x) < u_h(x) < \ubh(x)\}$ is open.  Furthermore, since the elements are $P_1$ here, if $E_h$ contains $x \in \mathring T$ in the interior of a cell $T\in \cT_h$ then it contains the entire cell interior ($\mathring T \subset E_h$).
The contact sets $\Cbotuh,\Ctopuh$ are therefore finite unions of closed mesh entities up to dimension $d$, i.e.~nodes, edges, and cells in 2D.  From similar reasoning, the free boundary $\Gamma_h$ is a finite union of closed entities, but only up to dimension $d-1$.

Like $u_h$ itself, the sets defined in \eqref{eq:fe:sets} are accessible \emph{a posteriori}.  The methods of the VIAMR library (Section \ref{sec:viamr}) use \emph{a posteriori} information to estimate the norm errors made in certain cells, or to (heuristically) identify cells near free boundaries where refinement should reduce location errors in these sets.  Tag-and-refine mesh refinement methods then add mesh cells to increase the resolution and reduce the numerical error, i.e.~$\|e_h\|_\cX$ or some other norm of $e_h = u_h-u$.  For VI problems, however, the simulation goal is often the accurate approximation of the sets \eqref{eq:contactsets}; attainability of this goal is clearly is problem-dependent.

For the classical obstacle problem, based on the Laplacian operator, Nochetto, Siebert, and Veeser (NSV) \cite{NSV03} have proposed properties/principles that \emph{a posteriori} estimators and associated adaptive procedures should exhibit.  Here is their list, adapted to the box-constrained general set-up in \eqref{eq:fe:admissible}\textendash\eqref{eq:fe:sets}:
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{(\alph{enumi})}

\smallskip
\begin{enumerate}
\item In the discrete contact sets $\Cbotuh,\Ctopuh$, estimators should be insensitive to the forcing term $\ell$, which should not dictate mesh quality.
\item In the discrete inactive set $E_h$, estimators should be insensitive to the obstacles $\lb,\ub$, and reduce to a corresponding PDE estimator.
\item Estimators should detect where the discrete solution is non-conforming, with respect to the continuum obstacles, i.e.~$u_h\notin \cK$, and refine accordingly.
\item If the residual measure $\sigma = F(u)-\ell$ is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure at a free boundary, then we expect strong refinement near there.  A piecewise-linear approximation of $\sigma$ cannot be very accurate in such a case.
\item It is not advisable to preadapt the mesh according to the data $\ell,\lb,\ub$ since the contact sets are unknown beforehand.
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
For the classical obstacle problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi}, our numerical results (Section \ref{sec:results}) show that the above properties are generally fulfilled,\footnote{The precise algorithm comparisons in Section \ref{sec:results} evaluate norms or effectivity versus number of degrees of freedom or run time.} at least qualitatively, by the major methods of this paper.  The VIAMR library includes implementations of the NSV estimators \cite{NSV03,NSV05}, which were proven to possess these desirable properties (in this classical case).  Furthermore, as documented separately in \cite{Bueler2026}, we have extended the \cite{NSV03} method from unilateral to box constraints, which nontrivially changes the cell classification used inside the estimator.  Thus the rigorous classical obstacle problem methods in references \cite{Bueler2026,NSV03,NSV05} are benchmarks for the other, more flexible but less rigorous, methods in the library.

Our target applications include obstacle problems with highly-nonlinear diffusion operators lacking uniform ellipticity.  For example, in the (unilateral) glacier application of Section \ref{sec:glacier} the unregularized diffusivity is identically zero in the contact set $\Cbotu$.  For such a degenerate problem a goal should be added to the above list, because for such a problem any refinement deep inside a contact set is wasted effort:

\smallskip
\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{5}
\item For degenerating obstacle problems of the appropriate type (Appendix \ref{app:blistering}), and subject to a sign convention on the source, cells should not be marked for refinement in computed contact-set regions.
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
\noindent For details see Section \ref{sec:glacier}.

Looking at the above list of desirable properties, and considering all our examples and results (Sections \ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:glacier}), we assert that properties (a), (b), and (d) are generally desirable, while properties (c), (e), and (f) may have only problem-dependent validity.  The original NSV goals (a)\textendash(e) are valid for obstacle problems where the operator satisfies a uniform-ellipticity condition, of course including the classical case for which the reliability of the  \cite{Bueler2026,NSV03,NSV05} estimators is proven.  However, rigorous estimator bounds are often not available for new classes of nonlinear operators.

However, users with nonlinear operators $F$, or gridded data for $\ell,\lb,\ub$, may not be able to achieve goal (c), they may disagree with (e), or they may expect (f):

\smallskip
\begin{itemize}
\item In model problems the obstacles $\lb,\ub$ are given by exact formulas, but in more realistic problems they may be given by in-exact observations on a pre-existing grid.  In that case the continuum admissibility goal (c) may be unattainable for $u_h$.  If the resolution for such gridded obstacles is coarse, relative to the (to-be-generated) computational meshes, then the provided obstacles should be regarded as $\lbh,\ubh$, and goal (c) abandoned or modified.
\item In the context of the glaciers obstacle problem (Section \ref{sec:glacier}), assertion (e) is at odds with the common sense of scientific users of the numerical glacier models.  They would assume that areas with positive source term ($\left<\ell,\varphi\right>\ge 0$ for $\varphi$ a hat function), where a glacier is certain to form, should be pre-refined to some degree, because in those areas a detailed fluid model must be solved.  In contrast, areas deep inside the $\ell<0$ region should be left coarse, because it is rare for a glacier to reach out to those areas.
\item Property (f) applies to the glacier problem, and practitioners would expect it.  It should be ignored for uniformly-elliptic operators.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip
\noindent In summary, the above NSV goals are a helpful guide for \emph{a posteriori} estimators, but different obstacle problems and nonlinear operators will limit what can be learned from them, as they are focussed on a classical model problem.  Users of the VIAMR library should always consider this goal list in choosing algorithms.

\section{\emph{A posteriori} error estimators for VIs} \label{sec:estimators}

FIXME strategy in this section: start with BR and BV estimators for elliptic PDEs.  mention GR.  the NSV estimators.  literature review classical obstacle problem estimators.  observe reconstructed $u_h$ makes sense.  then sketch strategy which we believe works in general (combination of UDO-type refine on free boundary plus PDE estimator in inactive set)

For $u\in\cX$ solving VI \eqref{eq:vi}, an interior condition PDE holds in the inactive set $E_h$ \cite{KinderlehrerStampacchia1980}.  Our AMR strategies (Section \ref{sec:viamr}) do \emph{a posteriori} refinement in the geometric vicinity of the free boundary, but we will also need to exploit \emph{a posteriori} error indicators for the interior condition to achieve convergence.  We consider two such PDE-type indicators.

\begin{example}  \label{example:gradrecovery}  Suppose $\cX_h=\CG_k$ is the continuous, piecewise-polynomial FE space of degree $k$, and consider $u_h\in\cX_h$.  Noting $\grad u_h$ is discontinuous, but well-defined on each element, define $\cY_h=\DG_{k-1}$ for the (scalar) discontinuous space with polynomial degree $k-1$, so that $\grad u_h \in \cY_h^d$ (vector-valued).  Suppose there is a linear operator $G : \cX_h \to \cX_h^d$, into the continuous space, called \emph{gradient recovery} (GR) \cite[Chapter 4]{AinsworthOden2000}, so that $G(u_h)\approx \grad u_h$ in some sense.  Over an element $K \in\cT_h$, the corresponding error indicator $\eta_K\ge 0$ is then
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gradrecoveryindicator}
\eta_K^2 = \int_K \left|G(u_h) - \grad u_h\right|^2.
\end{equation}
We also define $\eta^2 = \sum_{K\in\cT_h} \eta_K^2$.  For certain gradient recovery methods $G$, when they are applied to the Poisson equation, we find that $\eta \sim |u-u_h|_{H^1}$ (energy norm) as $h\to 0$ \cite[Theorem 4.4]{AinsworthOden2000}.
\end{example}

Our application of GR in Section \ref{sec:app} will simply use orthogonal projection in $L^2$ for the map $G$.  That is, $G(u_h) \in \cX_h^d$ is defined to be the minimizer of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gradrecoveryprojection}
J(w) = \int_\Omega |w - \grad u_h|^2\,dx.
\end{equation}
In this application $\cX_h = \CG_1$, $\grad u_h$ is in vector-valued $\DG_0$, and $G(u_h)$ is in $\cX_h^d$.

The second indicator, applied in Section \ref{sec:results} to classical problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi}, is a well-known \emph{explicit error estimator} \cite[Chapter 2]{AinsworthOden2000}.

\begin{example}  \label{example:br}  Suppose $u \in H^1(\Omega)$ solves the weak form Poisson equation $a(u,v) = \int_\Omega f v\,dx$ for all $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$, with $a(u,v)=\int_\Omega \grad u\cdot \grad v\,dx$ and $u=g$ on $\partial \Omega$.  Suppose that $u_h\in\cX_h=\CG_1$ solves the corresponding finite-dimensional weak form.  For each element $K \in \cT_h$, define $n_K$ as the unit outward normal vector on $\partial K$.  For a pair of cells $L,K$ incident to an edge $\gamma$, and any vector field $Z$ with traces $Z_L,Z_K$ on $\gamma$, let $\left\llbracket Z \cdot n \right\rrbracket = Z_L \cdot n_L + Z_K \cdot n_K$ be the jump of $Z$ on $\gamma$.  Given the (strong) residual $r(u_h)=-\nabla^2 u_h - f$ of the Poisson equation, well-defined within each element $K$, the Babu\v{s}ka--Rheinboldt (BR) \cite{BabuskaRheinboldt1979} error estimator is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:brestimator}
\eta_K^2 = h_K^2 \int_K |r(u_h)|^2\,dx + \frac{h_K}{2} \sum_{\gamma \in \partial K \setminus \partial \Omega} \int_{\gamma} \left\llbracket \grad u_h \cdot n \right\rrbracket^2\,dS,
\end{equation}
where $h_K$ is the diameter of $K$.  It can be shown \cite[Chapter 2]{AinsworthOden2000} that the energy error is bounded by $\eta^2 = \sum_{K\in\cT_h} \eta_K^2$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:brbound}
|u-u_h|_{H^1}^2 = \int_\Omega |\grad(u-u_h)|^2\,dx \le C \eta^2.
\end{equation}
Similarly the $L^2$ error can be bounded by an estimator, which replaces the powers in \eqref{eq:brestimator} by $h_K^4,h_K^3$, respectively \cite[Section 2.4]{AinsworthOden2000}.
\end{example}

For the results in Sections \ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:app}, whether $\eta_K$ is computed as in Example \ref{example:gradrecovery} or \ref{example:br}, we will treat the values $\eta_k$ as local element-wise error estimators.  The set $\{\eta_K\}$ will be thresholded to providing tagging of cells for refinement; see \cite[Section 4.2]{BangerthRannacher2003} for a discussion of techniques.  In our applications, all (computed) inactive cells satisfying $\eta_K \ge \theta \max \eta_K$, for $0<\theta<1$, will be tagged and refined.

The BR error estimator for the interior condition is explicit and easily-computed.  Ainsworth, Oden, and Lee \cite{AinsworthOdenLee1993} extend it to certain obstacle problems, but with heuristic aspects.  On the other hand, for certain PDE problems, alternative estimators are provided by the \emph{dual weighted residual} technique \cite{BangerthRannacher2003}.  These are defined using a particular quantity of interest and some approximately-computed nonlocal weights, essentially Green's functions of the adjoint PDE.  Suttmeier \cite{Suttmeier2008} has extended this weighted residual technique to certain VI problems, but it also requires heuristic steps even for the classical obstacle problem.  In this paper we avoid the complexity of such techniques, and instead take a pragmatic approach which combines refinement near a computed free boundary with application of a PDE-type error estimator, as above, within the computed inactive sets.

The first published analysis may be \cite{AinsworthOdenLee1993}, giving an error bound for the classical obstacle problem in terms of local functionals associated with each cell.  The monograph by Suttmeier \cite{Suttmeier2008} covers a broader class of problems, including elasticity. To our knowledge these approaches are not found in publicly-available implementations, nor are they as efficient as our strategies for computing high-resolution approximations to free boundaries.

Our focus in this paper is on AMR performance, not solver performance.  For all examples we used a fixed, VI-adapted, reduced-space Newton method with line search \cite{BensonMunson2006}, implemented in PETSc \cite{petsc-user-ref}.  Note that since the constraint $u \geq \psi$ makes VI problems nonlinear, an iterative solver is required even if the operator is linear as in \eqref{eq:classical:vi}.  Such a numerical method cannot converge quadratically until the active and inactive sets stabilize on the given mesh.  Then convergence will occur in one additional iteration, for a linear operator, or otherwise in a few iterations for a well-behaved nonlinear operator.

In summary, here are two principles for the AMR methods of this paper:
\renewcommand{\labelenumi}{\arabic{enumi}.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Relative to uniform refinement, they exhibit significant improvements in convergence rate.  This is measured by norms, or especially by free-boundary localization (geometrical) error (Section \ref{sec:results}),  per mesh degree of freedom.
\item Their implementations (\href{https://github.com/StefanoFochesatto/viamr}{{\small \texttt{github.com/StefanoFochesatto/viamr}}}) within the Firedrake \cite{Langeetal2016} FE library are parallel, well-documented, and easy-to-use.
\end{enumerate}

Our first two adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) methods, Algorithms \ref{alg:udo} and \ref{alg:vcd}, do targeted refinement near the free boundary.  They are of \emph{tag-and-refine} type.  The third \emph{metric-based mesh adaptation} approach \cite{Wallworketal2020}, Algorithm \ref{alg:avm}, is more expensive.

These methods all start from a computed solution $u_h \in \cK_h \subset \cX_h$ to problem \eqref{eq:fe:vi}.  Specifically we need only the mesh $\cT_h$, the obstacle $\psi_h \in \cX_h$, and $u_h$.  The following two concepts are fundamental.

\begin{definition} \label{def:nodalactive}
Denote the vertices of $\cT_h$ by $x_j$.  Given $u_h \ge \psi_h$ and a tolerance $\text{tol}>0$, the \emph{nodal active set indicator} is the unique $\nu_h\in\cX_h$ satisfying
	$$\nu_h(x_j) = \begin{cases} 1, & u_h(x_j) - \psi_h(x_j) < \text{tol} \\
	                             0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
\end{definition}

\begin{definition} \label{def:marking}
An \emph{element marking} of $\cT_h$ is a piecewise-constant indicator function $\oneh \in \DG_0(\cT_h)$ with values in $\{0,1\}$.
\end{definition}

For PDE problems,and element marking can be derived from an error estimator $\eta_K$, as in the previous Section, associated with a quantity of interest such as energy or $L^2$ norm error \cite{BangerthRannacher2003}.  However, a primary quantity of interest for obstacle problems is \emph{vicinity to the unknown free boundary}.  We do not associate this concept with a precise functional.  Instead Algorithms \ref{alg:udo} and \ref{alg:vcd} proceed heuristically to convert a nodal active set indicator into an element marking, with the goal of improving the approximation of the free boundary.

Queries of PETSc DMPlex objects \cite{Langeetal2016} will also be needed, so let us sketch how this class supports unstructured meshes.  A DMPlex object stores the topology (connectivity) of a dimension $d=1,2,3$ mesh.  Every mesh entity, regardless of dimension, is assigned a unique index.  Mesh connectivity is understood as a stratified directed acyclic graph, where a covering/incidence relationship between mesh entities determines the graph edges.  For example, a triangle within a 2D mesh is covered by its three edges, which are covered by their two endpoints (vertices).  Each stratum (``height'') in the DAG represents a dimensional class of mesh entity; for example the cells, edges, and vertices in a 2D mesh are at heights $0$, $1$, $2$.  Given the index $p$ of a mesh entity, the basic DMPlex queries \cite{petsc-user-ref} are $\text{\emph{cone}}(p)$, the set of indices of entities which cover entity $p$, and its dual $\text{\emph{support}}(p)$, the set of entities which are covered by $p$.  The transitive closure of \emph{cone} is $\text{\emph{closure}}(p)$, and that of \emph{support} is $\text{\emph{star}}(p)$.  In our application we need only the vertices in the element closure; we denote this as $\text{\emph{closure}}^\wedge(k)$.  Similarly, $\text{\emph{star}}_\vee(j)$ extracts only cells in the star of vertex $x_j$.

Now we can define the Unstructured Dilation Operator (UDO) method, Algorithm \ref{alg:udo}.  It first computes a nodal active set indicator $\nu_h$ from $u_h$.  Denoting the degrees of freedom in $\DG_0(\cT_h)$ by $x_k$, it then finds the set of indices $k$ of cells such that $0<\nu_h(x_k)<1$.  If $u_h \in \CG_1$ then this condition holds when the element is incident to both active and inactive vertices.  Then the method alternates closure and star on the marked element set $n$ times, expanding the marking by $n$ element layers, to generate a final element marking $\oneh$.  The motivation here is that if the approximation $u_h\approx u$ is good for a non-degenerate VI problem \eqref{eq:vi} then the true free boundary $\Gamma_u$ should pass through the cells indicated \emph{a posteriori} by $\oneh$.  In Sections \ref{sec:results} and \ref{sec:app} we will only consider $n=1,2$, as this much expansion seems to suffice for accurate representation of the free boundary after a few refinements.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
	\caption{Unstructured Dilation Operator (UDO) element marking}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require mesh $\cT_h$, solution $u_h \in \cK_h$, obstacle $\psi_h \in \cX_h$, tolerance $\text{tol} > 0$, and expansion parameter $n\ge 1$.
		\State Compute nodal active set indicator $\nu_h \in \cX_h$ for $u_h$.
		\State Find initial element index set $S_0$: \,$k\in S_0$ if $\nu_h(x_k) \in (0,1)$.
		\For{$i=0,\dots,n-1$}
		    $$S_{i+1} = \bigcup_{k\in S_i}\, \bigcup_{j\in \text{\emph{closure}}^\wedge(k)} \text{\emph{star}}_\vee(j)$$
		\EndFor \\
		\Return marking $\oneh \in \DG_0(\cT_h)$ of all cells with indices in $S_n$.
	\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:udo}
\end{algorithm}

While the UDO strategy explicitly manipulates indices, our second strategy, called Variable Coefficient Diffusion (VCD), Algorithm \ref{alg:vcd}, is based on continuum ideas.  Again the first step is to compute a nodal active set indicator $\nu_h \in \CG_1$.  This function is used as the initial condition of a time-dependent, variable-coefficient diffusion equation,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:diffusioneqn}
\frac{\partial s}{\partial t} = \Div\left(D \grad s\right), \qquad s(t=0) = \nu_h,
\end{equation}
with Neumann (natural) boundary conditions, a well-posed problem.  Clearly a solution of \eqref{eq:diffusioneqn} at $t>0$ is a smoothed form of $\nu_h$.  The diffusivity is set to the square of the element diameter $h_K$, namely $D=C h_K^2 \in \DG_0$, with $C=0.5$ by default.  The diffusion range is thus proportional to element diameter.  In fact \eqref{eq:diffusioneqn} is not solved exactly, or even very accurately, as we only compute $s_h\in\cX_h$ from a single backward-Euler step of duration $\Delta t = 1$, equation \eqref{eq:bestep} below.  The default solver for this linear and elliptic problem is four iterations of conjugate gradient, preconditioned by incomplete-Cholesky factorization \cite{Bueler2021}.  This inexpensive approximate solver has linear complexity in the vertices.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
	\caption{Variable Coefficient Diffusion (VCD) element marking}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require mesh $\cT_h$, solution $u_h \in \cK_h$, obstacle $\psi_h \in \cX_h$, tolerance $\text{tol} > 0$, and threshold interval $0 < \alpha < \beta < 1$.
		\State Compute nodal active set indicator $\nu_h \in \cX_h$ for $u_h$.
		\State For $h_K$ the element diameter, let $D=0.5 h_K^2 \in \DG_0(\cT_h)$.
		\State Approximately solve for $s_h\in\cX_h$, with natural boundary conditions:
		\begin{equation} \label{eq:bestep}
            s_h - \Div(D \grad s_h) = \nu_h
		\end{equation}
		\Return marking $\oneh \in \DG_0(\cT_h)$ of all cells such that $s_h(x_k) \in (\alpha,\beta)$.
    \end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:vcd}
\end{algorithm}

Figure \ref{fig:vcdillustration} illustrates how the VCD algorithm applies to a one-dimensional obstacle problem.  Note that the thresholds $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are key parameters, with defaults $\alpha=0.2$ and $\beta=0.8$.  Lowering $\alpha$ toward zero expands the marking further into the inactive set, away from the (computed) free boundary, while increasing $\beta$ toward one expands further into the active set.

It turns out to be helpful, e.g.~when solving the highly-nonlinear obstacle problem in Section \ref{sec:app}, to add a minimum element diameter $\hmin$ to Algorithms \ref{alg:udo} and \ref{alg:vcd}.  That is, after running one of the above Algorithms, we may un-set the element marking, $\oneh|_K=0$, of any element with $h_K < \hmin$.

From an element marking $\oneh$ we then apply \emph{skeleton-based refinement} (SBR) \cite{PlazaCarey2000} to generate a refined mesh.  cells with $\oneh=1$ are refined, and any other cells as needed to avoid hanging nodes.  Two SBR implementations are available, namely from PETSc DMPlex \cite{petsc-user-ref} and from the Netgen library \cite{Betteridgeetal2024}.  Only the latter is currently capable of 3D refinement.

Our third AMR method, called \emph{averaged-metric} (AMA; Algorithm \ref{alg:avm}), uses metric-based mesh adaptation \cite{Alauzet2010}.  In contrast to tag-and-refine methods, mesh adaptation generates a new mesh matching resolution and complexity targets.  Adaptation is driven by an \emph{a posteriori} metric ﬁeld, defined as a continuous, matrix-valued function $M_h:\Omega \to \RR^{d\times d}$ with each value $M_h(x)$ a symmetric and positive-definite matrix.  Such a metric contains local information on distances, areas, and volumes \cite{LoseilleAlauzet2011}.  From the metric the mesher itself generates a unit mesh \cite{Alauzet2010}.  In the original space the refined mesh has variable edge lengths and element aspect ratios.

AMA again starts from $u_h$ and $\psi_h$.  The first metric is isotropic, and it is computed from the gradient of a smoothed nodal active set indicator, namely $s_h$ from the VCD method (Algorithm \ref{alg:vcd}).  The second metric is anisotropic, computed as an approximate Hessian of $u_h$ via a Hessian-recovery technique \cite{Alauzet2010}.  (Actually the absolute value of the Hessian is used, which well-defined for a symmetric matrix \cite{Wallworketal2020}.)  These metrics are in the matrix-valued $\CG_1$ FE space, with metric normalization constants computed from target complexity and element diameter bounds in a standard manner \cite{Wallworketal2020}.  By construction, the first metric should generate small cells near the free boundary, while the second should reduce FE approximation error in the inactive set, according to the standard interpolation theory \cite{Ciarlet2002}.  In AMA the final metric is a weighted average of the two metrics.  This final metric is both anisotropic and free-boundary focussed, and it implies refinement in both the active and inactive sets.  Our implementation calls the Animate library (\href{https://github.com/mesh-adaptation/animate}{{\small \texttt{github.com/mesh-adaptation/animate}}}) to construct, normalize, and average the metrics, and the Pragmatic library \cite{Gormanetal2012} for meshing at the last step.  Note that steps 2 and 3 in Algorithm \ref{alg:avm} use the target complexity and element diameter bounds parameters.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
	\caption{Averaged-metric (AMA) mesh adaptation}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require mesh $\cT_h$, solution $u_h \in \cK_h$, obstacle $\psi_h \in \cX_h$, target complexity $N$, element diameter bounds $0<\hmin<\hmax$, and averaging weight $0\le \gamma \le 1$
		\State Compute $s_h \in \cX_h$ from Algorithm \ref{alg:vcd}, using $u_h$ and $\psi_h$.
		\State Compute normalized isotropic free-boundary metric: $M_1(x)=c_1 |\grad s_h(x)| I_{d\times d}$.
		\State Compute normalized anisotropic metric from Hessian: $M_2(x)=c_2 |H u_h(x)|$.
		\State Average the metrics: $M(x) = \gamma M_1(x) + (1-\gamma) M_2(x)$. \\
		\Return new mesh $\tilde \cT_h$ which is unit with respect to $M(x)$.
    \end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:avm}
\end{algorithm}

The right-hand image in Figure \ref{fig:threeballmeshes} (Introduction) shows an AMA result on the ``ball'' obstacle problem; see Figure \ref{fig:activesizes}, middle.  Note that iterating the AMA method, even while holding the target mesh complexity constant, can be worthwhile because the increased resolution near the free boundary allows the \emph{a posteriori} metric to become more effective.  A key idea when iterating AMA is that cross-mesh interpolation \cite{Farrelletal2009} provides a high-quality initial iterate on the new mesh.  Because of the nontrivial computations needed in metric-based methods \cite{Alauzet2010,Wallworketal2020}, at high resolution one AMA iteration is notably more expensive than an iteration of UDO or VCD.

All three Algorithms run in parallel under the MPI protocol used by Firedrake \cite{Langeetal2016} and PETSc.  However, only UDO produces results which are independent of the number of processes.  The default preconditioned Krylov solver in VCD is slightly-dependent on process count \cite{Bueler2021}.  Choosing a direct solver for problem \eqref{eq:bestep} would give the VCD method process-independence, but it would also reduce scalability.

Consider the first two integrals in bound \eqref{eq:falktwo} from the point of view of AMR.  Their size is determined partly by the action of $F$ on the continuum obstacle, and partly by the source term $\ell$.  However, because they are over the active set $A_u$, neither integral, nor the third $A_u$ integral, requires mesh refinement so as to improve the quality of the FE solution, \emph{as long as the mesh and solver have accurately located the free boundary}.  This observation suggests why the primary goal of AMR for VIs should be to generate close approximation $\Gamma_u^h\approx \Gamma_u$.  Computation expended on better approximation of the data $\psi$ in $A_u$ can be avoided if the free boundary is accurately resolved.  This is easiest to exploit within the class of unilateral obstacle problems described in Appendix \ref{app:blistering}, which includes the $\psi=0$ case of the classical obstacle problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi} and the glacier problem of Section \ref{sec:app}. In order to measure the quality of the approximate sets \eqref{eq:fe:sets}, versus the exact sets \eqref{eq:classical:sets}, and to determine rates of geometric convergence, the VIAMR library computes a distance between sets.  Our implementation of \eqref{eq:jaccard} below is based on Firedrake's supermesh concept \cite{Farrelletal2009}, so it is computable for sets $S,T$ which are unions of cells from different meshes, and/or for sets defined by algebraic (conditional) expressions in the mesh coordinates.

\begin{definition} \label{def:jaccard}
The \emph{Jaccard distance} \cite{LevandowskyWinter1971} between measurable sets $S,T \subset \Omega$ is
\begin{equation}
d(S,T) = 1 - \frac{|S \cap T|}{|S \cup T|}, \label{eq:jaccard}
\end{equation}
where $|\cdot|$ is Lebesgue measure, with $d(S,T)=0$ by definition if $|S \cup T|=0$.
\end{definition}

We will use Jaccard distance to compare active sets.  If $d(S,T)=0$ then $S$ and $T$ geometrically agree up to a set of measure zero, and for a computed active set $A_u^h$, defined by an element marking (Definition \ref{def:marking}), we will report $d(A_u^h,A_u)$ when $A_u$ is exactly known.

A second goal for numerical solutions of obstacle problems is to avoid wasted effort in the active set, once the free boundary is well-resolved.  As already discussed, if $\Gamma_u^h \approx \Gamma_u$ is a good approximation then the obstacle data itself, namely $\psi$, can be used to represent the solution in the computed active set $A_u^h$.  This motivates a definition.

\begin{definition} \label{def:preferred}
Suppose the original VI problem \eqref{eq:vi} has exact obstacle $\psi \in \cX\cap C(\bar\Omega)$.  Consider a computed solution $u_h$ to the approximating problem \eqref{eq:fe:vi}, with computed active set $A_u^h$, a union of closed cells.  We define the \emph{preferred approximation} $\tilde u_h$ as the measurable function
\begin{equation} \label{eq:preferredapprox}
\tilde u_h(x) = \begin{cases} \psi(x), & x\in A_u^h \\ u_h(x), & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

For a classical obstacle problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi}, the preferred approximation $\tilde u_h$ is in $L^2(\Omega)$.  It is generally discontinuous, even when $\cX_h=\CG_k$, thus $\tilde u_h \notin H^1(\Omega)$.  However, $\tilde u_h$ is defined \emph{without} reference to the exact solution to problem \eqref{eq:vi}; only the continuum data $\psi$ is referenced.  The $L^2$ error relative to $\tilde u_h$ has a clear decomposition over the sets defined in \eqref{eq:classical:sets} and \eqref{eq:fe:sets}:
\begin{equation}
\|u-\tilde u_h\|_2^2 = 0 + \int_{A_u\setminus A_u^h} |\psi - u_h|^2 + \int_{A_u^h\setminus A_u} |u - \psi|^2 + \int_{E_h\cap E_h^h} |u-u_h|^2.  \label{eq:preferreddecomp}
\end{equation}
The leading zero comes from the integral over $A_u\cap A_u^h$, where $u=\tilde u_h=\psi$.  The next two terms are small if the free boundary has been accurately located ($\Gamma_u^h \approx \Gamma_u$), the goal of AMR in Section \ref{sec:viamr}.  The final term is small if inactive-set refinement is effective (Section \ref{sec:inactive}).  The next example shows that our methods converge in norm \eqref{eq:preferreddecomp}.

\begin{example}  \label{example:ball}  Consider the ``ball'' classical obstacle problem shown in Figure \ref{fig:ball} and Figure \ref{fig:activesizes} (middle), which has a known exact solution \cite[Chapter 12]{Bueler2021}.  Algorithms \ref{alg:udo} (UDO) and \ref{alg:vcd} (VCD) were applied to this problem, combined with inactive-set marking based on the BR error estimator \eqref{eq:brestimator}.  These methods, denoted UDO$+$BR and VCD$+$BR, avoid refinement in the active set, except near the estimated free boundary.  We also applied mesh adaptation Algorithm \ref{alg:avm} (AMA), setting mesh complexity targets to closely-match the number of cells from the other Algorithms; note that AMA refines everywhere.  The experiment started from a coarse uniform mesh over the square domain, and applied 11 levels of AMR, so that the number of cells increased by four orders of magnitude.  We also compared uniform refinement.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:jaccball}, our AMR methods are clearly superior to uniform refinement when evaluated by active set Jaccard distances $d(A_u^h,A_u)$.  At higher resolutions, all three AMR methods reduce this distance an order of magnitude below uniform refinement.  While AMA produces the best meshes by this measure, its runtime is about an order of magnitude higher at comparable mesh complexity (not shown).

\section{Application to determining glaciated land areas} \label{sec:glacier}

In this section we apply our AMR techniques to a model for the steady geometry of a glacier, over a given bedrock topography and subject to a given climate.  In particular, this model solves for which land is covered by ice.  Though glacier ice will cover any land on which snowfall exceeds melt, ice flow expands the glaciated area out to a free boundary, the glacier margin, which can only be found from conservation equations.  In fact, any fluid layer subject to surface processes which add or remove mass is governed by a related model \cite{Bueler2021b}.  In these VI problems, which generally are \emph{not} of optimization type, the unknown fluid thickness must be nonnegative, the operator is nonlinear, and the blistering property (Appendix \ref{app:blistering}) applies.

Let $\Omega \subset \RR^2$ be a fixed land region, with bedrock elevation $b \in C^1(\Omega)$ and a surface mass-balance \cite{GreveBlatter2009} function $a:\Omega \times \RR \to \RR$.  The value of this climatic function is the annually-averaged rate of ice accumulation (snow) minus melt and runoff.  If $a$ depends on the surface elevation $s$ then we suppose that $a(x,y,s)\in L^\infty(\Omega)$ for any $s \in L^\infty(\Omega)$, and additionally that $a$ is Lipschitz continuous in $s$.

We use an ice flow approximation called the \emph{shallow ice approximation} \cite{GreveBlatter2009}, derived by a small aspect ratio argument from mass and momentum conservation, in its simplest isothermal and non-sliding case.  Applying the standard exponent for the shear-thinning flow law of ice \cite{GreveBlatter2009}, we have a VI for admissible transformed ice thickness functions $u \in \cK = \{u \in \cX\,:\,u\ge 0 \text{ and } u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}$, in the Banach space $\cX = W^{1,4}(\Omega)$ \cite{JouvetBueler2012}.  The thickness itself is $H=u^{3/8}$, while the surface elevation is $s=H+b=u^{3/8}+b$.  The model is a VI based upon a ``tilted'' variation of the $p$-Laplacian operator \cite{JouvetBueler2012}:
\begin{equation}
\int_\Omega \Gamma |\grad u + \bm{\beta}(u)|^2 (\grad u + \bm{\beta}(u)) \cdot \grad (v-u) - \tilde a(u) (v-u)\,dx \ge 0 \label{eq:sia:vi}
\end{equation}
for all $v \in \cK$.  Here $\Gamma>0$ is an ice softness constant, the tilt $\bm{\beta}(u)=\frac{8}{3} u^{5/8} \grad b$ is a nonlinear multiple of the bed gradient, and $\tilde a(u)=a(x,y,u^{3/8}+b)$ denotes the surface mass balance.  If $a$ has no $s$ dependence then we may treat it as the source term, and write ``$F(u)[v-u]\ge\ell[v-u]$'' as in \eqref{eq:vi}, but otherwise we simply set $\ell=0$ in \eqref{eq:vi}.  If also $\grad b=0$ then \eqref{eq:sia:vi} defines a nonlinear operator $F(v)[w]$ which is $4$-coercive; see Example \ref{example:flatsia}.  Regarding the mathematical theory of \eqref{eq:sia:vi}, existence holds for any $b$ when $a$ is independent of $s$ \cite{JouvetBueler2012}.  Simple cases of \emph{non}-existence are known when $a$ depends on $s$ \cite{Jouvetetal2011}, but our examples below have slow increase of snowfall with elevation \cite{GreveBlatter2009}, which avoids this issue, and we suppose the solutions below are unique.

The strong-form interior condition for \eqref{eq:sia:vi} is a conservation equation, namely $\Div \bq = \tilde a$ over $E_h$, where the flux is given by
\begin{equation}
\bq = - \Gamma |\grad u + \bm{\beta}(u)|^2 (\grad u + \bm{\beta}(u)).
\label{eq:sia:interior}
\end{equation}
At the free boundary (glacier margin) the solution $u$, the solution gradient $|\grad u|$, and the flux $\bq$ all go to zero.  On the other hand, observations confirm that the gradient of a glacier's surface has large magnitude as one approaches the margin from the ice side, a property which emerges from \eqref{eq:sia:vi} when one differentiates the associated surface elevation $s=u^{3/8} + b$.  Generally $|\grad s| \to \infty$ at the glacier margin.

We apply a conforming, continuous, piecewise-linear FE method to \eqref{eq:sia:vi}, over unstructured triangulations.  The FE problem is either solved directly by the same VI-adapted Newton solver used in Section \ref{sec:results}, namely \texttt{vinewtonrsls} in PETSc \cite{petsc-user-ref}) , or with an added outer Picard-type iteration over the tilt $\bm{\beta}(u)$ \cite{JouvetBueler2012} and source $\tilde a(u)$, with Newton as the inner iteration.  The Newton steps are solved using Firedrake's default direct linear solver.  Very similar results are obtained when these iterations both converge, but the Picard-based solver is more robust when $|\grad b|$ is large or $\tilde a$ depends on $u$.

We consider two particular problems, each over a square domain $\Omega=(0,L)^2$ with $L=1800$ kilometers.  The first ``dome'' problem has a flat bed, elevation-independent source, and a known exact solution \cite{Bueler2016}; this is used for verification and to compare AMR algorithms.  The second problem has a bumpy bed and an elevation-dependent surface mass balance function.

\begin{example} \label{example:dome}
The dome problem was solved with 13 levels of refinement from an initial, uniform $h_0=360$ kilometer mesh to a final mesh with glacier margin resolution of $h_{13}\approx 30$ meters.  Both UDO and VCD methods  (Algorithms \ref{alg:udo} and \ref{alg:vcd}) were applied, with gradient recovery (GR; Example \ref{example:gradrecovery}) marking in the inactive set,\footnote{Note that the argument for BR marking given in Section \ref{sec:inactive} does not apply here.} and the results were very similar.  Because the obstacle is $\psi=0$, the preferred \eqref{eq:preferredapprox} and standard numerical solutions are the same.  Figure \ref{fig:domenormresults} shows relative $H^1$ errors in the solution ($\|u-u_h\|_{H^1}/\|u\|_{H^1}$) and absolute $L^\infty$ errors in the corresponding ice thickness ($\|H-H_h\|_{L^\infty}$), versus number of cells.  The latter errors are much more responsive to AMR because the ice thickness gradient is singular at the free boundary.  That is, Figure \ref{fig:domenormresults} right is primarily reporting (lateral) margin location errors.  In fact, similar to the Jaccard distances reported in Example \ref{example:ball}, Figure \ref{fig:domeradiusresults} shows the maximum radial error for the numerical margin.  We see that our AMR methods are quite efficient for free-boundary localization, compared to uniform refinement.

\begin{example} \label{example:realistic}
The next example is more realistic.  It uses a bumpy bedrock topography $b(x,y)$, constructed from a finite sum of sinusoids \cite[Example 8.4]{BuelerFarrell2024}, and a surface mass balance function $a(s)$ which depends only on surface elevation; see the source code for details.  An important parameter in the formula for $a(s)$ is the equilibrium line altitude (ELA) \cite{GreveBlatter2009}, denoted $s_0$, with $a>0$ above this altitude and $a< 0$ below.  To illustrate this parameter, Figure \ref{fig:transect} shows the topography along a transect $x=\hat x$, the red line in Figure \ref{fig:glacier}, with three $s_0$ values superimposed.  Net snowfall $a>0$ occurs on a solution-dependent set $S=\{(x,y)\,:\,u(x,y)^{3/8} + b(x,y) > s_0\}$, and glaciation then extends beyond $S$ because of flow: $E_h \supset S$.  The difficult nonlinearities in model \eqref{eq:sia:vi} include this elevation-dependent surface mass balance, but also the $u^{5/8}$ dependence in the tilt $\bm{\beta}(u)$.  Among the effects of the latter is the tendency of ice to pool in bedrock elevation lows.  Our primary goal in this example is to generate an accurate map of glaciation, i.e.~of the inactive set $E_h$.  Related goals are to compute the ice volume $V=\int_\Omega u^{3/8}\,dx\,dy$, and to find the number of connected components of $E_h$, that is, the number of glaciers in the given climate.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

It is well-known that inequality-constrained obstacle problems are intrinsically nonlinear, thus that solution methods must be iterative.  At a basic level our AMR methods simply extend the mandatory iteration to the generation of sequences of well-adapted unstructured meshes.

The UDO and VCD Algorithms from Section \ref{sec:viamr} combine two relatively-simple tagging strategies based \emph{a posteriori} on the numerical solution:

\smallskip
\begin{enumerate}
\item assuming $u_h$ and $\psi_h$ can estimate the true free boundary $\Gamma_u$, mark nearby cells on both sides of the computed free boundary $\Gamma_u^h$, and
\item assuming $u_h$ approximates the true solution $u$ in the computed inactive set $E_h^h$, mark cells in this set using PDE-type error estimates (Section \ref{sec:inactive}).
\end{enumerate}

\smallskip
\noindent AMA Algorithm \ref{alg:avm} uses the same essential heuristics to generate a metric, which enables mesh adaptation.

Our AMR strategies are tied to the \emph{a priori} theory in Section \ref{sec:vifem}.  On the other hand, we have no direct, quantitative theory of how they reduce numerical error.  Observe that \emph{i)} has nothing directly to do with the regularity or residual of $u_h$; refinement is geometrical in the domain.  On the other hand, \emph{ii)} could be done by methods of arbitrary sophistication.  For example, dual weighted residual methods \cite{BangerthRannacher2003,Suttmeier2008} could be applied, although their adjoint-type weights are incapable of communicating information between disconnected components of the inactive set, the number and geometry of which are only found at solution time.  (Example \ref{example:blisters} is an example.)

For classical obstacle problems, the mesh sequences built by our AMR techniques allow the bulk of the Newton iterations to occur inexpensively on the coarse early meshes. However, for harder problems like the glaciation obstacle problem in Section \ref{sec:app}, the solver and AMR components strongly interact.  Nontrivial solver iterations are needed on intermediate meshes, to find and stabilize the free boundary.

An important extension of our work would be to find effective refinement strategies for time-dependent obstacle problems and parabolic VIs.  Also, performance could be improved by integrating AMR into a multilevel solver, though this is also for future research.  For example, the solver in \cite{BuelerFarrell2024} uses coarse meshes to make large corrections, including geometrically to the free boundary, and so a combination with AMR promises highly-scalable solutions of difficult obstacle problems.

\section{Obstacle problems with the blistering property} \label{app:blistering}

In a classical, unilateral obstacle problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi} an upward force $f(x)>0$ may occur in the active set $A_u$.  Physically speaking, in such a case the upward force was insufficient to lift $u$ off the obstacle $\psi$.  However, in certain problems this situation cannot occur.  The hypotheses of Lemma \ref{lem:blister} hold for classical problems \eqref{eq:classical:vi} when $\psi=0$, for the porous dam saturation free-boundary problem \cite[for example]{AinsworthOdenLee1993}, and for the glacier problem in Section \ref{sec:app}.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:blister}
Suppose that $\psi=0$ and $\ell[v] = \int_\Omega fv\,dx$ for $f\in L^2(\Omega)$.  Suppose also that the operator $F$ is given by measurable densities,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:densitiesform}
F(w)[v] = \int_\Omega \phi(w(x),\grad w(x)) v(x) + \Phi(w(x),\grad w(x)) \cdot \grad v(x)\,dx,
\end{equation}
satisfying $\phi(0,0)=0$ and $\Phi(0,0)=0$.  Then for $u$ solving VI \eqref{eq:vi}, $f\le 0$ a.e.~in $A_u$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $S\subset A_u$ be a Borel set, and denote its indicator function by $\chi_S \in L^\infty(\Omega)$.  By a density argument and the assumptions on $\phi$ and $\Phi$, the operator value $F(u)[\chi_S]$ is well-defined, \emph{and zero}, because $S$ is in the active set.  Thus by Lemma \ref{lem:measure},
\begin{equation*}
0 \le d\mu_u(S) = F(u)[\chi_S]-\ell[\chi_S] = 0 - \ell[\chi_S] = -\int_S f\,dx.
\end{equation*}
This shows $f\le 0$ a.e.~with respect to Lebesgue measure.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}
For a unilateral obstacle problem \eqref{eq:vi} with source term $\ell[v] = \int_\Omega fv\,dx$ and $f\in C(\bar \Omega)$, we say that the \emph{blistering property} holds if $A_u \subset \{x \in \Omega\, :\, f(x)\le 0\}$ \cite{JouvetBueler2012}, that is, if the conclusion of Lemma \ref{lem:blister} holds.
\end{definition}

To explain the language, if $f(x)>0$ for some $x\in\Omega$ then, assuming this property, the inactive set $E_h$ must be non-empty, and $x\in E_h$.  That is, even a small upward force ``blisters'' the membrane off the obstacle.

Any classical problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi} with a smooth obstacle $\psi\in C^2(\bar\Omega)$ can be transformed into one with the blistering property.  Let $\tilde v=v-\psi$, thus $\tilde\cK = \{\tilde v\in\cX\,:\,\tilde v\ge 0 \text{ and } \tilde v|_{\partial\Omega}=g-\psi|_{\partial\Omega}\}$.  Integration-by-parts shows \eqref{eq:classical:vi} is equivalent to finding $\tilde u = u -\psi \in \tilde \cK$ so that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:classical:vi:blister}
\int_\Omega \nabla \tilde u \cdot \nabla(\tilde v - \tilde u) \ge \int_\Omega \left(f+\grad^2\psi\right)(\tilde v - \tilde u) \quad \text{ for all } \tilde v \in \tilde \cK.
\end{equation}
For VI problem \eqref{eq:classical:vi:blister}, if $x\in A_{\tilde u} = \{\tilde u(x)=0\}$ then $\tilde f(x)= f(x)+\grad^2\psi(x)\le 0$.

For blistering-property problems, AMR costs can be reduced by systematically avoiding refinement in the computed active sets.  From a computed solution $u_h$, one identifies cells $K\in \cT_h$ such that $K \subset A_u^h$.  (In our implementation we require that every node in the closure of $K$ is active, according to some tolerance.)  The degenerate case must be excluded, so we also require $K \subset \Omega_- = \{x\in \Omega\,:\,f(x) < 0\}$; note this uses only the data of the problem.  Then for $K\subset \Omega_- \cap A_u^h$ no refinement is needed \emph{if} $K$ is geometrically far from the free boundary.  However, if later refinements move the free boundary to be incident to $K$ then refinement of $K$ becomes appropriate.
